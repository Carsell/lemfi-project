# Findings

Generated by `python run.py`. Every number is computed from the data, not typed in by hand.

## Data quality

- 600 duplicate transaction_id — deduplicated on load. Left in place they would inflate corridor volume and double-count alerts
- 40 users with no home_country — kept as 'Unknown'
- 150 transactions where amount x exchange_rate does not reconcile to converted_amount — quarantined, not corrected, because we do not know which of the three fields is the wrong one

## Results

1. **Volume is concentrated in a few corridors.** 75,737 transfers move £68.3m over six months, excluding 150 FX-quarantined rows from value. The largest corridor, UK to NG, carries 30% of value on its own, and the top three carry 59%. Average transfer size varies 1.6-fold across corridors, so the impact of a single global amount threshold should be checked by corridor rather than assumed.

2. **The rule misses more than it finds, and still buries the team.** Suspicious activity runs at 1.04% of transfers. The simulated baseline rule flags 3.7%, catching 25% of genuinely suspicious transfers at 7% precision. In plain terms: 2,832 alerts over six months, of which 2,636 are false, and 589 suspicious transfers are missed entirely. That is roughly 16 alerts a day, about 0.4 full-time reviewers at an illustrative 40 reviews per day, and more than three quarters of their day spent on transfers that turn out to be fine. Whether that is the right trade depends on what a missed case costs relative to a review, and that number should come from the business rather than from me.

3. **New accounts are slightly riskier, but the alert trade-off needs testing.** Transfers from accounts under 14 days old are suspicious 1.36% of the time against 1.01% for everyone else. That is a real but small elevation, and 8% of alerts involve an account under 14 days old. A threshold sensitivity test by account age would show whether the condition can be tightened without losing too much recall.

4. **A single-transaction rule cannot see structuring, and there is structuring.** Aggregating to one row per customer per day finds 28 days where a user made three or more transfers, each below the £10,000 scenario threshold, together totalling more than it — £0.7m in all. In this controlled simulation the daily rule recovered 26 of 26 planted structuring days (100% recall) with 2 false-positive days (93% precision). The simulated single-transaction rule caught 0% of them. No amount of tuning the amount threshold fixes this, because every individual transfer is unremarkable and the rule only ever looks at one at a time. The fix is a daily aggregate per customer. These figures validate the scenario logic; they are not estimates of real-world detection performance.

5. **Verification method and verification outcome are different things, and only one of them is about risk.** The method a user verifies through decides whether they get through: 83% pass via Document Upload against 54% via Utility Bill. That is an operations and conversion problem worth fixing on its own terms. The *outcome* is a different matter and it is a real risk signal: suspicious activity runs at 2.90% among users whose verification failed against 0.72% among those who passed, roughly 4 times higher. Those two facts pull in opposite directions. This aggregate comparison does not establish that changing a verification method would change later risk, so that decision needs an experiment or a controlled cohort analysis rather than a causal claim from this chart.
