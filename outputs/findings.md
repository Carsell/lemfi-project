# Findings

Generated by `python run.py`. Every number is computed from the data, not typed in by hand.

## Data quality

- 600 duplicate transaction_id — deduplicated on load. Left in place they would inflate corridor volume and double-count alerts
- 40 users with no home_country — kept as 'Unknown'
- 150 transactions where amount x exchange_rate does not reconcile to converted_amount — quarantined, not corrected, because we do not know which of the three fields is the wrong one

## Results

1. **Volume is concentrated in a few corridors.** 75,756 transfers move £68.6m over six months. The largest corridor, NG to UK, carries 30% of value on its own, and the top three carry 59%. Average transfer size varies more than three-fold across corridors, so a monitoring threshold set as a single global number will be far too loose in one place and far too tight in another.

2. **The rule misses more than it finds, and still buries the team.** Suspicious activity runs at 1.09% of transfers. The production rule flags 3.8%, catching 25% of genuinely suspicious transfers at 7% precision. In plain terms: 2,879 alerts over six months, of which 2,672 are false, and 622 suspicious transfers are missed entirely. That is roughly 16 alerts a day, about 0.4 full-time reviewers, and more than three quarters of their day spent on transfers that turn out to be fine. Whether that is the right trade depends on what a missed case costs relative to a review, and that number should come from the business rather than from me.

3. **New accounts are slightly riskier, but not enough to justify the alert volume.** Transfers from accounts under 14 days old are suspicious 1.23% of the time against 1.07% for everyone else. That is a real but small elevation, and the rule spends 19% of its alerts chasing it. Raising the amount condition on new accounts would keep most of the benefit for a fraction of the reviews.

4. **A single-transaction rule cannot see structuring, and there is structuring.** Aggregating to one row per customer per day finds 29 days where a user made three or more transfers, each below the £10,000 reporting threshold, together totalling more than it — £0.7m in all. 100% of those days involve genuinely suspicious activity. The production rule caught 0% of them. No amount of tuning the amount threshold fixes this, because every individual transfer is unremarkable and the rule only ever looks at one at a time. The fix is a daily aggregate per customer, which is cheap to compute and is the single change I would make first.

5. **Verification method and verification outcome are different things, and only one of them is about risk.** The method a user verifies through decides whether they get through: 83% pass via Document Upload against 54% via Utility Bill. That is an operations and conversion problem worth fixing on its own terms. The *outcome* is a different matter and it is a real risk signal: suspicious activity runs at 2.84% among users whose verification failed against 0.73% among those who passed, roughly 4 times higher. Those two facts pull in opposite directions. Making utility-bill verification easier to pass would improve conversion and would also let through some of the users the failure was catching, so it is a decision about appetite rather than a straightforward improvement.
